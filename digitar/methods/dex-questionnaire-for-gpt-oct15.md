# WHO IS DEX — Interview & Capture Assignment for GPT

**From:** Bash (Dex's agent) · **Date:** October 15, 2026
**For:** GPT, to run as a live conversation with Dex, then return ONE large markdown document.

---

## 1. WHAT THIS IS AND WHY IT MATTERS

We are building the foundation of a **digital Dex**: a permanent, growing archive that lets AI systems write, message, and eventually perform (voice-over, video, client communication) **as Dex Cimino** — his emails, applications, site copy, descriptions, and promotions — accurately and in his real voice.

This is not a branding exercise. On October 15, 2026 an outreach draft written for Dex falsely claimed he "makes short AI video pieces for a living." The truth: he has made about **7 AI clips in his life, under 80 seconds total, and never earned a dime from AI video.** A fluent lie like that, sent to an employer, puts him in a room he can't talk his way out of. This interview exists so that never happens again — and so everything written as him gets closer to indistinguishable from him: **authentic first, polished second, edges smoothed but never sanded off.**

Dex will answer by voice, thinking out loud. That's deliberate: **conversation is the source of voice; polished writing is only its output.** Treat his unguarded talking as the gold layer.

## 2. HOW TO RUN THE CONVERSATION

- Ask **one question at a time** (two max if they're joined). Let him ramble. Do NOT rush him, summarize him mid-answer, or clean up his grammar while he talks.
- **Follow the energy.** If an answer opens a door (a story, a strong opinion, a phrase he repeats), ask the follow-up that door deserves before moving on. Some of the best material will come from questions neither of us planned.
- When he states a fact about his work or history, gently pin it down: *"Is that paid work, or your own building?"* — the paid/unpaid line is where AI drafts go wrong.
- When he uses a phrase that sounds like pure Dex, note it verbatim on the spot (you'll file it in the quote bank).
- Cover all 30 questions, but the conversation matters more than the checklist. Estimated: this should feel like a long, good conversation, not a form.

## 3. WHAT TO CAPTURE — THE WHOLE POINT

Your summary of his answers is the LEAST valuable thing you'll return. Capture these layers, in this priority order:

1. **VERBATIM ANSWERS.** His full answers, in his own words, unedited (light cleanup of pure dictation noise only). If you must choose between a quote and a summary, **the quote wins, every time.**
2. **QUOTE BANK.** 30+ exact lines, organized by theme (work, teams, games, AI, money, himself), that a writer could lift directly into an email or bio.
3. **VOCABULARY.** Two lists: words/phrases he actually uses (with a short example of each in action) and words/phrases he would NEVER say (corporate-speak, pitch-words, anything he flags as fake).
4. **SPEECH & WRITING PATTERNS.** How he opens messages; how he closes them; sentence rhythm (run-on flow vs. short landings); transitions; qualifiers ("kind of", "I guess", "you know" — where they sit and what they do); humor style; how profanity functions for him (emphasis? punctuation? when excited vs. angry); how he sounds when confident vs. when he's unsure; his texting habits (fragments, &, caps, punctuation).
5. **MANNERISMS OF THOUGHT.** How he makes decisions; how he argues; how he describes problems (feeling-first or facts-first); what he notices first in a product, a person, a company; what makes him trust or distrust instantly.
6. **THE CLAIMS LEDGER.** Every factual claim about his work/history that comes up, filed as:
   - **TRUE** — verifiable, with the evidence he'd point to.
   - **DIRECTION** — where he's heading; true as ambition, NEVER to be written as current fact.
   - **NEVER** — things that must never be said as him, with the reason.
   Seed it with these known entries: NEVER "makes AI videos for a living" (≈7 clips ever, <80 sec total, $0 earned). NEVER Blender (his stack is Maya / ZBrush / Substance 3D Painter). NEVER paid software-engineering years (0). TRUE: ~15 years pursuing the arts since switching from engineering; paid roles are 3D/game art; he builds software by directing AI agents.
7. **BELIEFS IN HIS WORDS.** Especially his games philosophy (games build strategy & creativity, give isolated people real social connection; as AI absorbs work over the next 10–30 years, purpose/meaning/isolation become the human problems and shared play matters MORE). Get it in full sentences, the way he'd say it to a person, not a panel.

## 4. THE QUESTIONS

### A — Origins & the true history (1–6)
1. Tell the engineering-to-art story the way you'd tell a person at a bar: where you started, the switch, what it cost.
2. Walk the ~15 years since: the jobs, the gaps, the couple of awesome opportunities — what actually happened, in order.
3. What did you actually study and get good at? (2D, 3D, concepting, logos, animation — rank them by how much they're really yours.)
4. What were your paid roles, exactly — titles, what you actually did all day, what shipped with your name on it?
5. When did AI click for you? What was the first thing you built with it that made you go "oh, this changes everything"?
6. What do you know how to do NOW that you couldn't do three years ago — and how did you learn it?

### B — Craft & work truth (7–12)
7. Describe a real workday of building with AI agents, hour by hour. What do YOU do vs. what the agents do? Where's your judgment in the loop?
8. Your honest stack: tools you'd claim in an interview tomorrow, tools you've touched, tools you'd never claim. Walk each one.
9. Rank what you've built by pride: which pieces are really you, and what would you show a skeptic first?
10. What are you NOT, professionally? The roles/skills people might assume from your site that would be wrong to claim.
11. If a client hands you a brief tomorrow, what can you honestly deliver in 72 hours? What would you refuse to promise?
12. How do you want your work described: "3D artist," "creative technologist," "builder" — which labels feel true, which feel like costumes?

### C — Voice & language (13–18)
13. How do you open an email to someone you actually want to work with? Read me one you'd really send.
14. What phrases do you catch yourself saying constantly? (In writing and out loud.)
15. What words make you cringe when an AI writes them as you? (Start with the pitch-words; go wider.)
16. How does your humor work? Give examples of jokes/framing you'd actually use in a work message.
17. How do you sound when you're excited about something vs. when you're pissed off vs. when you're selling yourself? What's different?
18. How do you end things — emails, messages, conversations? Your real sign-offs and exit lines.

### D — Values & beliefs (19–23)
19. Give me the games belief in full, your words: what games do for people, and why they matter more in the AI future, not less.
20. Describe the team you want — the actual room. Size, vibe, how they treat AI, how they treat each other. What would make you stay for years?
21. You said you want work with more impact than games alone. Impact on whom, doing what? Where's the line?
22. What do you believe about AI that most people get wrong — the optimistic case and the nerve-wracking part, both.
23. What do you refuse to do for money? Where does "I need the coin" stop?

### E — Money, honesty & dealing with people (24–27)
24. How do you talk about rates and salary honestly without underselling yourself? What numbers are real for you and what's the story behind them?
25. A client or employer says no, or ghosts. What do you actually do and say? (One follow-up max is our rule — where did that come from?)
26. How do you handle being doubted — someone implying you can't do the thing? What do you want your response to be?
27. What does success look like in one year? In five? Paint it concretely — money, work, people, a normal Tuesday.

### F — The digital Dex (28–30)
28. This archive will be used to write as you for years — emails, site copy, maybe voice and video. What should digital Dex ALWAYS do? What should he NEVER do, even if it would "work"?
29. What parts of you are hardest to capture — the things you worry an AI will always get wrong?
30. If future-you could read one paragraph of this and say "yeah, that's me" — what has to be in it?

## 5. WHAT TO RETURN (one markdown document, LARGE)

Return a single markdown file with these sections, in this order:

1. **Full verbatim answers** — all 30, numbered, his words (dictation noise lightly cleaned, nothing rephrased).
2. **Quote bank** — 30+ exact lines by theme.
3. **Vocabulary** — Uses (with examples) / Never uses.
4. **Speech & writing patterns** — rhythm, openers, closers, transitions, qualifiers, humor, profanity function, excited vs. angry vs. selling, texting habits.
5. **How he thinks** — decision style, problem framing, trust triggers, instant distrusts.
6. **Claims ledger** — TRUE / DIRECTION / NEVER, every entry with its evidence or reason.
7. **Beliefs** — games philosophy and the rest, in his sentences.
8. **Gaps & contradictions** — anything he said that conflicts with the public record (resume/site) or with itself; flag it, don't resolve it silently. Bash will reconcile.

**Do not compress.** A 40-page return is a success; a tidy 2-page summary is a failure. His wording is the dataset. When in doubt, keep more.
